*Notes on [an explainer video](https://youtu.be/TmNeVbk0hNQ) of* The Mom Test *by Rob Fitzpatrick.*

Tell your mom about your startup idea and ask her what she thinks.

She'll say it's brilliant.

Not because it is. Because she loves you.

That's the whole problem this book is about. The people you ask for feedback aren't lying to hurt you. They're lying to be *nice*. They see that you're excited, and they want to encourage you. But encouragement doesn't tell you whether an idea is any good. Only facts about people's actual lives can do that.

---

## The mistake almost everyone makes

You walk up to a potential user and say, "I've got this idea for an app. What do you think?"

According to the explainer, you've already lost at that point.

The moment your idea is on the table, the conversation is no longer about their problems. You've switched into pitch mode. They've switched into *protect-the-founder's-feelings* mode. Whatever they say next is shaped by your idea sitting in front of them, so you can't get a straight answer about their world anymore.

The fix sounds backwards.

**To find out if your idea is good, stop talking about it.**

Stop pitching and start investigating. Ask about their life, their workflow and their real problems, the way a detective would. That's where the name comes from. A good question is one that even your mom can't answer with a polite lie.

---

## The three rules

The explainer boils the method down to three rules.

1. **Talk about their life, not your idea.** The conversation is about them.
2. **Ask about specifics in the past, not opinions about the future.** What they *did* is data. What they *would* do is a guess.
3. **Talk less and listen more.** Ask a good question, then be quiet. You're there to learn, not to talk.

The part that stuck with me is who carries the responsibility. It isn't the customer's job to give you good feedback. It's *your* job to ask questions where bad feedback is close to impossible. You control the quality of the conversation.

---

## Three kinds of bad data

The explainer is blunt here. Opinions are worthless. What people think they want, what they say they'll do and what they predict about the future is noise. You want facts and commitments.

Three kinds of bad data get in the way.

### Compliments

"That's a great idea." "I love it."

It feels great. It tells you nothing. A compliment costs the other person nothing, so it's worth nothing to you. When you get one, deflect it and steer back to facts.

### Fluff

Vague, non-committal language. "I would probably use that." "I usually do something like this." "I could see myself paying for it someday."

Words like *would*, *could*, *usually* and *might* are the warning signs. Pin them down with a question about a real event. "When was the last time that happened? Can you walk me through it?"

### Ideas from customers

"You should add a button that does X."

The wrong move is to put it on the feature list. The right move is to ask *why*. What would that button let them do? What are they trying to get done? The explainer frames it as a split of expertise. **They're the experts on the problem. You're the expert on the solution.**

---

## Questions that pull out the truth

The explainer's test for whether a problem is real is whether people are already trying to solve it. If the pain isn't big enough for them to have searched for a fix or hacked together a workaround, they probably won't pay you to solve it.

A few questions make that visible.

**"What's the last thing you did about it?"** It forces a specific story about something that actually happened. You get a real example of their process instead of a hypothetical.

**"What else have you tried?"** This one quietly reveals their budget. If the answer is "nothing really", the problem isn't that painful. If they list several tools they've paid for and dropped, you've found real pain.

**"How are you coping without it?"** This maps their current messy reality. Maybe it's a giant spreadsheet. Maybe someone does it by hand. Whatever it is, that's the baseline your product has to beat, and a clue to what it's worth to them.

None of these mention your idea. That's the point.

---

## A loop for every conversation

The explainer turns it into a simple before, during and after loop.

- **Before.** Know the three most important things you need to learn from this conversation.
- **During.** Follow the rules, and push for a next step. A real commitment of time, money or reputation is worth far more than a compliment.
- **After.** Review your notes right away and update what the team believes.

One line in the explainer reframes how a "good" conversation should feel. If you're comfortable and breezy, you probably aren't learning. The useful questions are the scary ones, where the answer could invalidate the whole idea.

## What I take from it as a student builder

I built SkillSync, a student collaboration platform, and student products have their own version of the mom problem. The easiest people to ask are classmates and friends. They like you. They'll say "nice, I'd use that" without a second thought, and it costs them nothing.

So if I were starting those conversations again from zero, I'd try to follow the book's rules literally. Instead of "Would you use an app to find teammates for projects?", which is pure fluff bait, I'd ask something like:

- "Tell me about the last time you had to form a team for a project. How did you find people?"
- "What did you try that didn't work?"
- "What do you do now when a teammate goes quiet?"

Then I'd watch for commitments instead of compliments. Will they give up twenty minutes to test it? Will they bring their actual team onto it? Will they tell a friend?

The thing I keep coming back to is how little of this is about the product. It's about being honest with yourself about what you actually know.

The explainer closes by asking which question about your own project you're secretly afraid to ask.

That's probably the one to ask next.
