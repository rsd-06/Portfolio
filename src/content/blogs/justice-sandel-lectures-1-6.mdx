*Notes on [Justice: What's The Right Thing To Do?](https://youtu.be/kBdfcR-8hEY) by Michael Sandel, Harvard University. Episodes 1 to 6.*

I went in expecting a philosophy lecture.

What I got was a packed hall of students raising hands, standing up to defend a position, and watching that position get turned against them ten minutes later.

That's the format. Sandel tells a story, takes a poll, asks someone to explain their vote, then changes one detail. The principle that felt obvious a moment ago suddenly doesn't. Only after the room has argued itself into a corner does he bring in the philosopher who saw it coming centuries earlier.

You can't watch it passively. Every case is aimed at you, and you notice your own answer flipping.

Each episode packs two lectures. Here's what each one covers, as the class argued it.

---

## The moral side of murder

*Episode 01. [Watch it here](https://youtu.be/kBdfcR-8hEY).*

It opens with the trolley problem. You're driving a trolley with no brakes. Five workers are ahead. You can steer onto a side track with one worker. Most of the room would turn.

Then the twist. You're on a bridge instead, next to a large man. Push him onto the track and he stops the trolley. One dies, five live. Same arithmetic.

Almost nobody would push.

Students try to explain the difference. Pushing feels like murder with your own hands, while steering feels like choosing between outcomes already in motion. Sandel keeps pressing. What if you only had to open a trap door? Still feels wrong. A doctor who saves five moderately injured patients instead of one critical one seems fine. A surgeon who takes organs from a healthy man napping next door to save five does not.

Out of this mess come two families of moral reasoning:

- **Consequentialist.** Morality lives in the results. Five beats one.
- **Categorical.** Some acts are wrong in themselves, whatever the outcome.

Jeremy Bentham's utilitarianism is the big example of the first. Immanuel Kant is the big example of the second. The course plays them against each other.

The second lecture tests Bentham on a real 19th-century case, *The Queen v. Dudley and Stephens*. The yacht Mignonette sank in the South Atlantic, and four men ended up in a lifeboat with two cans of turnips. On the nineteenth day, Captain Dudley proposed drawing lots to decide who would die. A sailor named Brooks refused. The next day Dudley and Stephens killed Richard Parker, the seventeen-year-old cabin boy, who was already ill and had no family. They survived on him until a ship rescued them, then pleaded necessity at trial.

Most of the class votes guilty. The interesting part is *why*. Some say murder is murder. Others say it would be different if Parker had consented, or if they'd held a fair lottery. Sandel turns this into three questions the course has to answer. Why is murder categorically wrong? Why would a fair procedure justify its outcome? What moral work does consent actually do?

He also warns the room that philosophy makes the familiar strange, and you can't unsee it. The easy escape is skepticism, the shrug that everyone has their own opinion. His reply is that we already live some answer to these questions every day.

## Putting a price tag on life

*Episode 02. [Watch it here](https://youtu.be/0O2Rq4HJBxw).*

Bentham's idea is simple. We are governed by **"two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure,"** so the right thing to do, for a person or a lawmaker, is whatever maximizes the overall balance of happiness over suffering.

Today that logic often goes by the name cost-benefit analysis. Sandel's examples are uncomfortable on purpose.

**Philip Morris in the Czech Republic.** The tobacco company commissioned a study showing that smoking produced a net gain of about $147 million for public finances. Part of the "benefit" was that smokers die early, saving on pensions, health care and elderly housing. The company later apologized.

**The Ford Pinto.** Ford knew the fuel tank could explode in rear collisions. The fix cost $11 a car, $137 million in total. On the other side, Ford counted 180 deaths at $200,000 each, plus injuries and burned cars, for about $49.5 million. So it skipped the fix. The jury was appalled.

One student says the problem isn't that the number was too low. It's putting a number on a life at all. Another says you have to, because decisions get made either way.

Two objections to utilitarianism come out of this:

1. **It can trample individuals.** If enough Romans enjoy watching Christians thrown to lions, does their pleasure outweigh the victims' agony?
2. **It assumes every value fits on one scale.** In the 1930s a psychologist named Thorndike asked young people on relief how much they'd need to be paid to have a tooth pulled, eat a live earthworm, or live the rest of their life in Kansas. Kansas came out most expensive. The absurdity is the point.

The second lecture brings in John Stuart Mill. Bentham had said that, for equal amounts of pleasure, **"pushpin is as good as poetry."** Mill argues some pleasures are higher, and his test is to ask people who have experienced both which they prefer.

So Sandel runs it live with a Hamlet soliloquy, a clip of *Fear Factor* and a clip of *The Simpsons*. Most of the class enjoyed The Simpsons most. Most also said Shakespeare was the higher experience. That gap between what we prefer and what we judge worthier is exactly where Mill's test wobbles.

Mill also argues that justice is the most sacred part of morality, because respecting rights makes society better off in the long run. Sandel leaves the question open. Is Mill quietly stepping outside utilitarianism to get there?

The episode ends with Bentham himself, preserved in a glass case at University College London, where board minutes record him as **"present but not voting."**

## Free to choose

*Episode 03. [Watch it here](https://youtu.be/Qw4l1w0rkjs).*

Sandel first pushes on Mill. If a doctor refuses to harvest a healthy patient's organs only because it would scare people away from checkups, is that the right reason? Or does the person deserve respect in their own right?

That opens the door to strong theories of rights, starting with libertarianism and Robert Nozick. We are separate individuals with a fundamental right to liberty, so the state may do very little. Libertarians reject three things most modern states do:

- **Paternalist laws** that protect you from yourself, like seat belt and helmet laws
- **Morals legislation** that tries to make citizens virtuous
- **Redistribution**, taxing the rich to help the poor

What's left is a minimal state for defense, police and courts.

For Nozick, you can't judge a distribution of wealth by its shape. You ask whether the original holdings were acquired fairly and whether everything since changed hands through free exchange. If both hold, the result is just, however unequal.

Sandel makes it concrete with Bill Gates and Michael Jordan. A utilitarian would tax them in a heartbeat, since a little means nothing to them and a lot to people at the bottom. A libertarian calls that coercion.

Then the chain of reasoning that makes this episode memorable. Taxing my earnings takes the fruits of my labor. That's like taking my labor itself. Claiming my labor is forced labor. And if the state owns part of my labor, it owns part of *me*. Underneath it all sits one premise, **self-ownership**.

A student named Victoria goes straight for that foundation. Maybe you don't fully own yourself once you choose to live in a society.

In the second lecture, a "team libertarian" of three students answers the class's objections. The poor need the money more? Need doesn't justify violating a right, and needing something isn't the same as deserving it. Taxes in a democracy have consent? One vote among millions isn't deciding what to do with your own property. The successful owe society a debt? They already repaid it through what they gave others in free exchange.

The episode closes by tracing self-ownership to its source, John Locke.

## This land is my land

*Episode 04. [Watch it here](https://youtu.be/MGyygiXMzRk).*

At first Locke looks like the libertarian's best ally. He believes in natural rights to life, liberty and property that exist before any government, and that no government can override.

His account of property is the famous part. You own yourself, so you own your labor. Mix your labor with something unowned, like picking fruit or tilling land, and it becomes yours, provided there's enough, and as good, left for others.

The catch is that Locke's rights are **unalienable**. Sandel compares them to a non-transferable ticket. It's yours, but you can't sell it. For Locke, you can't take your own life or sell yourself into slavery. So you don't own yourself in the absolute way libertarians want.

The class pushes on the property theory too. A student named Rachelle argues it conveniently justifies settlers taking land from Native Americans who didn't enclose it. Others reply that, on Locke's own terms, hunting on and using common land might count as labor, so the argument could cut the other way.

The second lecture turns to **consent**. Why leave the state of nature? Because everyone is their own judge and enforcer, and people judging their own cases overreact. So we agree to form a government and accept majority rule.

Here Locke gets slippery. He says government can't take your property without your consent, but also that taxes need only the consent of the majority. Sandel's reading is that *that* there is property is natural and protected. *What counts* as property, and as taking it, is for government to define.

What makes a law legitimate is that it's general, not arbitrary. Singling out Bill Gates to fund a war would be illegitimate. A general tax wouldn't. One student argues a conscription lottery works the same way.

So libertarians get two disappointments. Unalienable rights mean you don't fully own yourself. And a government built on consent turns out to be less limited than they hoped.

Sandel also notes a darker angle. Locke wrote that in the beginning "all the world was America," and he was a colonial administrator. A theory of property that needs no consent may have suited that context.

## Hired guns

*Episode 05. [Watch it here](https://youtu.be/8yT4RZy1t3s).*

Now the question is the limits of consent in markets.

Sandel starts with the US military missing recruitment targets during the Iraq war. He offers three fixes. Raise pay, bring back the draft, or hire mercenaries. The class overwhelmingly picks higher pay.

Then history. In the Civil War you could be drafted and pay someone to go in your place. People ran newspaper ads offering $500 or more. Andrew Carnegie reportedly paid a substitute less than he spent on cigars in a year. Most of the class calls this unjust, for two reasons:

1. **Coercion.** A poor man taking the money isn't choosing freely. He's taking the best of a few bad options.
2. **Civic duty.** Military service shouldn't be just another job, because it's tied to citizenship.

Then a student named Emily turns the discussion. If the Civil War system is unfair because the poor end up fighting, doesn't today's all-volunteer army do the same thing, nationwide? Sandel adds that "volunteer army" is a misnomer. It's a *paid* army. Follow market choice consistently and you end up with mercenaries. Take patriotism seriously and it argues for a draft.

The second lecture moves the question to reproduction. A college newspaper ad offered $50,000 for an egg from a tall, athletic, high-scoring donor. A commercial sperm bank accepted under 5% of applicants.

Then **Baby M**. Mary Beth Whitehead signed a contract to carry a child for William and Elizabeth Stern for a $10,000 fee. After the birth she wanted to keep the baby. Most of the class said enforce it. A deal is a deal.

The objections were that she couldn't have known, before the child existed, how she would feel, and that the whole thing looks a lot like selling a baby. The New Jersey Supreme Court used both. It voided the contract, gave custody to the father in the child's interest, restored the mother's rights, and said some things in a civilized society money can't buy.

Philosopher Elizabeth Anderson sharpens the second objection. Some goods are wrongly valued if we treat them only as things to use. Respect, love, honor and awe are other ways of valuing, and a market pushes them aside.

## Mind your motive

*Episode 06. [Watch it here](https://youtu.be/8rv-4aUbZxQ).*

Finally, Kant, whom Sandel calls the hardest philosopher in the course.

Kant agrees we feel pain and pleasure. But what sets us apart is our capacity for reason and free action, and his idea of freedom is strict. Chasing your desires isn't freedom, because you didn't choose your thirst. Sandel's example of the opposite of freedom is the old Sprite slogan, **"obey your thirst."**

To be free is to act **autonomously**, by a law you give yourself. Acting on desire or outside forces is **heteronomy**. A falling billiard ball isn't free. It just follows physics.

So what gives an action moral worth is the **motive**, not the result. Only acting from duty, doing the right thing because it's right, counts.

Kant's example is a shopkeeper who gives an inexperienced customer the correct change only because cheating might hurt his reputation. Right action, wrong reason, no moral worth. Sandel finds modern versions, like a Better Business Bureau ad selling honesty as the most profitable policy.

A thirteen-year-old spelling bee champion confessed that he'd actually misspelled the winning word. Part of his reason, he said, was **"I didn't want to feel like a slime."** Does that ruin it? A student named Judith says no. Other feelings can come along, as long as duty is the reason you act.

Kant states his supreme principle, the **categorical imperative**, in two formulas covered here:

1. **Universal law.** Act only on a principle you could will everyone to follow. A false promise to borrow $100 fails, because if everyone did it nobody would believe promises at all.
2. **Humanity as an end.** Treat people, yourself included, never merely as a means, always also as an end.

A student says the first one sounds like consequences. Mill made the same criticism. Sandel's answer is that it's a test, not a reason. It checks whether you're putting your own needs above everyone else's.

Another student asks whether buying food from a cashier counts as using them. No, says Sandel, as long as you deal with them in a way that respects their dignity.

## What I take from it

The biggest effect of these six lectures wasn't learning who said what. It was noticing how fast my own reasoning shifts when one detail of a case changes.

I build systems and train ML models. A lot of that work is, in effect, Bentham with a loss function. Pick an objective, add everything up into one number, maximize it. Usually that's fine. Often it's the only workable approach.

But the Pinto memo stuck with me. Its problem was never the arithmetic. It was what got counted, and the decision that a life could be one more entry in the sum.

Thorndike's survey shows the same trap from another side. Once everything sits on one scale, you can rank anything against anything, and the ranking will be confidently absurd.

So the lesson isn't "don't optimize." It's narrower:

- **Know what your single number hides.** A model that does well on average can still fail a small group badly. That's the minority-rights objection, applied to an accuracy score.
- **Some limits aren't weights.** Some constraints shouldn't be traded away for enough benefit. They belong outside the objective, as hard rules.
- **The reason matters, not just the result.** A system that behaves only because misbehaving would be noticed is Kant's shopkeeper. Worth remembering when we judge models by outputs alone.

None of these lectures hands you a final answer, and Sandel doesn't pretend they do. What they gave me is a habit. When a trade-off looks obviously right, I change one detail and check whether I still believe it.
