*Notes on [a personal video essay on despair and pain](https://youtu.be/DNGXsbZHMMI).*

A quick note before anything else. This post talks about despair and suicidal feelings. It stays away from details, but if you're not in a place to read about that today, it's fine to close the tab. There's a helpline at the bottom.

---

## Where it starts

The essay opens with the narrator's own story. Fifteen years earlier, he says, he wanted to die, and one night he came close to acting on it.

He didn't. Something he describes as a deeper desire to survive pulled him back, and he went home alive with a strange mix of feelings.

I won't retell more than that. What matters for the rest of the essay is how he describes the state he was in.

He compares it to standing under a huge waterfall. The pain surrounds you and presses down so hard that you can't move. Someone nearby might shout at you to step out, without realising you physically can't. He calls it *drowning by paralysis*.

Then, quietly, he says he has grown a lot since, and no longer stands under that waterfall.

That one sentence carries the whole essay. It's written by someone who got out.

---

## The name and what the essay is really about

The title phrase belongs to Emil Cioran, the Romanian philosopher who published a book called *On the Heights of Despair* in 1934. But this essay doesn't walk through Cioran's book. It's a personal reflection on pain, and the thinkers it actually leans on are Peter Wessel Zapffe, Byung-Chul Han and Franz Kafka.

So these notes follow what the narrator says, not Cioran.

---

## The elk with the giant antlers

The first idea comes from Zapffe's essay *The Last Messiah*.

Zapffe points to the Irish elk, which grew antlers so enormous it couldn't hold its head up to feed properly. In Zapffe's telling, the very feature that set it apart helped finish it off.

The narrator borrows this for the individual. Humans have such a capacity for empathy and sensitivity that it can turn against us. We hurt for a flower that dies. We hurt for things that can't feel pain themselves.

I find that a kind framing. It says the problem is not that you're weak. It's that you feel a lot.

---

## Why "it could be worse" doesn't work

This section rang truest to me.

When someone is low, there's always a voice saying it could be worse. You have a roof. Others have it harder.

The narrator's answer is simple. Suffering happens *inside*. Outside events may trigger it, but the thing that turns them into pain is internal. What hurts one person may not hurt another.

And other people's pain doesn't cancel yours. Misery may love company, he says, but company doesn't heal misery. If anything, empathy adds the world's sadness on top of your own.

He also says there's no true zero for pain. Even in a perfect life you would still feel sorrow over something. So treating suffering as a score you can compare is a mistake.

Then the hardest part. Deep pain resists words. Asking a depressed person "what do you have to be sad about?" is a bad question because it often has no answer. The pain is real, but the language for it doesn't exist. That leaves people alone with it, sometimes unable to explain it even to themselves.

---

## Trauma and the broken fin

He's honest that he used to roll his eyes at the word *trauma*. He changed his mind. However casually the word gets used, the thing itself is real.

Trauma, he says, doesn't only hurt the part of life it touches. It can reshape how you see the whole world. If you believed the world was basically safe and then something cruel happened, you lose the event *and* the belief.

He also takes on "what doesn't kill you makes you stronger". His counterexample is a dolphin whose fin is cut by a boat propeller. It keeps swimming and the wound heals. But it isn't stronger. It swims slower, hunts worse, and hides whenever it hears a motor.

People can be like that. Wounds of the spirit don't have to heal for life to go on. Sometimes we just go on limping.

---

## You can't plug every hole

So should we build a life that keeps pain out?

The narrator says that's impossible. His image is a wooden boat with holes drilled all over it. Plug one and the water pushes harder through the rest.

His position is careful. Don't seek pain. Don't romanticise suffering. Don't abandon people in crisis. But accept that pain is part of being alive. It's neither good nor bad. It just *is*.

He's clear that a life arranged around avoiding pain completely is more dangerous than accepting it, because when pain inevitably comes, you have nothing ready to cope with it. He connects this directly to his own past despair.

So the goal isn't a pain-free life. It's learning to understand pain and to cope with it.

---

## What pain can do

Then he borrows from Byung-Chul Han's *The Palliative Society*.

Han argues that pain and love are tied together. Anything that can't hurt you probably doesn't matter much to you. Whatever we love is exactly what can wound us. The narrator calls the capacity for pain a kind of barometer for what is real in our lives.

He adds Kafka, who wrote in a letter that his writing was a reward for being, as he put it, "pinched, beaten and ground nearly to dust". For Kafka, writing and suffering couldn't be separated.

The narrator's own version is that pain can push us into the new. Without discomfort we would happily stay as we are forever. Change usually starts when something hurts.

He keeps repeating the caveat, though. Pain isn't good. It's just not wholly bad either.

---

## Not a mountain, a valley

The ending is where the essay turns toward living.

He used to see life as a mountain to climb and conquer. Pain was a stumble that cost you progress. Life itself was a fight against pain.

He has let that go.

Now he sees life as a valley you walk through. As a whole, it isn't for or against you. But it's full of small wonderful things. Dandelion seeds in the air. Grass under your feet like a cat's paws in your lap. Sunlight that fades into night and always comes back. Bees that might sting you, not because of anything you did, but because that's what bees do.

He knows the valley ends at a tree line some day. His hope is to have appreciated it so much by then that he can welcome the change of scenery.

---

## What I take from it

I'm wary of writing anything that sounds like advice here, so I'll keep it small.

What stays with me is the shift from mountain to valley. As a student, almost everything around me is framed as a climb. Exams, rankings, the next milestone. That framing makes every bad week feel like falling. The valley framing doesn't deny the bad weeks. It just stops treating them as failure.

And the dolphin. Not every wound makes you stronger, and you don't owe anyone a growth story. You can just keep swimming.
