*Notes on [a video about John Calhoun's Universe 25](https://youtu.be/LuCans9euxY).*

Eight mice. Four males, four females.

Unlimited food. Unlimited water. No disease, no predators, no threats of any kind.

A few years later, every one of them and every one of their descendants was dead. And not one death came from anything a vet could have diagnosed.

That's Universe 25, and the video I watched calls it the most disturbing experiment ever run on a society. I don't know about "most". But I haven't stopped thinking about it.

---

## The man and the question

John Calhoun was an ethologist, someone who studies animal behaviour. The video says his whole career circled one question.

> What happens to a society when nothing is hard anymore?

By 1968 he had been chasing it for about two decades. He had built earlier versions in a quarter-acre plot in Maryland, in barn enclosures, and in lab rooms. Twenty-four of them, each a slightly different paradise for rats or mice. No scarcity, no predators, no escape.

And in every one, he saw the same arc. The population boomed. Violence spiked. Mating behaviour turned distorted and hyperactive. Then everything collapsed into apathy, and the colony died.

The 25th was meant to be the clean, final version.

---

## The setup

He built it in a federal research lab in Maryland. A 9-foot square enclosure, walls lined with stacked nesting boxes reached by mesh tunnels from floor to ceiling. Calhoun called them *walk-up apartments*.

Food hoppers never ran empty. Water bottles never ran dry. The temperature stayed constant, nesting material was everywhere, and the cages were cleaned by hand on a schedule. The founders came from the healthiest stock at the National Institutes of Health breeding colony.

He estimated the space could hold about 3,800 mice. It never came close.

---

## The phases

### Settling in

For the first 104 days, almost nothing happened. The mice explored, learned the layout and sorted out who slept where.

### The boom

Then the litters came, and the population doubled roughly every 55 days. By day 315 there were more than 600 mice, climbing over each other on the ramps and fighting for nest space.

This is where it starts to break.

In a normal colony, when there are too many mice for the territory, the extras leave and find new ground. In Universe 25 there was no new ground.

### The split

Young males with nowhere to go and nothing to do drifted to the centre of the pen. They lay around in listless heaps. When attacked, they didn't fight back. They absorbed it, walked a few feet, and lay down again.

A second group of males went the other way. Frantic, fast, ignoring courtship rituals, forcing their way into other males' nests, undeterred even by violence.

Females who couldn't find a place in the social order withdrew too. They moved to the highest apartments, lived alone and stopped reproducing.

Calhoun also described what he called the **behavioral sink**. Food was equally available everywhere, yet the mice crowded compulsively around a few feeders. They wouldn't eat alone. Most of the enclosure sat nearly empty while a handful of stations were mobbed.

### The mothers

Then parenting broke down. Defending a nest in that crowd took constant effort. The territorial males got exhausted and stopped protecting their families, so the mothers had to defend nests while nursing.

They began abandoning pups around the enclosure. Some stopped recognising their own young. In the worst stretches, mothers ate their offspring. The video says that in some sections, fewer than one in 20 newborns survived to weaning.

Calhoun's verdict was that the colony's social fabric was effectively dead, even though the bodies were alive.

### The beautiful ones

The few pups who survived grew into something Calhoun had never seen.

These males never fought and never tried to mate. They ate, slept and groomed themselves obsessively. Because they never fought, their fur stayed perfect. They moved through the colony as if no other mouse existed.

He called them **the beautiful ones**. They looked like the healthiest mice in the enclosure. Functionally, they had opted out of the species.

### The end

The population peaked at about 2,200 on day 560. The last successful conception happened on day 920. After that, no new mice were born, and the colony simply aged out.

According to the video, the last mouse died on 23 May 1973, four years and ten months after the lid closed.

Calhoun wrote that the mice died twice. First the death of the spirit. Then, years later, the death of the body.

## What it was actually about

The popular reading was simple. Comfort kills. Abundance makes you soft. The beautiful ones became a go-to metaphor for anyone warning about modern decay.

The video's point is that this isn't what Calhoun concluded.

His argument was about **roles**. When far more individuals are capable of filling meaningful roles than there are roles available, he wrote, violence and disruption follow. The food never ran out. What ran out was a reason for each mouse to take part.

The fighters had no one worth fighting. The young grew up with nothing to inherit. Comfort didn't kill them directly. It removed the friction that gave each mouse a place.

And Calhoun wasn't a doomer. The video says he spent the rest of his career trying to find what you could put *back* into a paradise to keep it working. His proposed answer had a long name, **ideational generativity**. As physical space runs out, you expand into conceptual space. New ideas, new networks, new roles that didn't exist before.

---

## A note on how far it goes

It's worth being honest about the limits here.

Universe 25 was one enclosure of mice, and humans are not mice. Later researchers have debated how far its results generalise, and whether they say anything reliable about human cities or societies at all. The vivid images, especially the beautiful ones, travel much further than the careful caveats.

So I treat it as a striking case study and a useful metaphor, not as proof about people.

---

## What I take from it

The metaphor I keep coming back to is not "comfort is bad". It's that a system can have every resource it needs and still fail if it has no meaningful roles to give its members.

It's easy to picture a small version of this in a team. Give people infrastructure, free food and a nice office but nothing real to own, and they either drift or start fighting over the few things that matter. The fix is not adding hardship. It's creating more real work worth doing.

Calhoun's own answer is the one I like best. Keep making new reasons to be here. As a student about to start a career, that reads less like a warning and more like a job description.
