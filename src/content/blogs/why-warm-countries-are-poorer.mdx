*Notes on [Why Warm Countries Are Poorer](https://beyondtweets.substack.com/p/why-warm-countries-are-poorer) by Giovanni Foglietta (Beyond Tweets), which builds on a piece by Tomas Pueyo.*

Look at a map of income per person and a pattern jumps out. On average, warm and humid regions are poorer than cold ones.

That's an uncomfortable thing to read when you live in a warm country. So I read the essay carefully, mostly to see whether it was saying "hot places are doomed". It isn't.

The author's thesis has two halves, and the second half matters as much as the first. Warm regions are poorer on average, **but it is not fate**. Harsh climates forced people to plan and to build institutions that scale. Easy climates let societies get away with less planning, and sometimes let elites extract instead of build.

The essay's key idea is a *web of constraints*. No single factor explains the gap. Several smaller ones stack on top of each other.

Here are the strands, as I understood them.

---

## Heat lowers productivity

Above a certain temperature, people work worse, both physically and mentally. The essay lists examples like more factory downtime, lower test scores in classrooms without air conditioning, less construction output and more call-centre errors on hot days.

The headline number it cites is from economists Dell, Jones and Olken. On average, each extra 1°C of annual temperature goes with roughly 8.5% lower GDP per capita. Inside countries, hotter municipalities also tend to be poorer. The essay adds that physical labour productivity can drop about 4% per degree above 27°C.

There are indirect effects too, like more crime in heat and worse sleep.

The counterpoint the author raises is Singapore and Dubai. Both are hot. Both are rich. They offset the climate with capital, technology and infrastructure.

---

## Humidity is the hidden half

Heat with dry air is one thing. Heat with high humidity is worse. When the dew point is high, sweat can't evaporate, so the body can't cool itself. Hard physical work becomes physiologically hard. The essay points out that outsiders often misread this as laziness.

Humidity also wears things down faster. Mould, corrosion and machinery wear all speed up. Crops and stored food spoil sooner. Disease spreads more easily.

That's one reason the author treats air conditioning as a development technology, not a luxury.

---

## Disease burden

Malaria, yellow fever, dengue and parasitic worms all thrive in warm, wet places.

The essay cites the World Bank figure that malaria cuts sub-Saharan Africa's GDP by about 1.3% a year, and that incomes in malaria-endemic areas are under a third of those in malaria-free regions. Childhood infections can cause lasting harm. Families tend to have more children, which leaves less to spend on each child's education.

Cold has a hidden benefit here. Frost resets the cycle for many pathogens every year.

One detail I didn't know is that malaria was once common in Italy, Spain and the American South. Its eradication in the mid-20th century coincided with those regions catching up faster.

---

## Winter forces you to plan

This is the strand the author finds most persuasive, and I agree it's the most interesting.

Winter stops food production for months. If you don't store and share surplus, you die. So cold societies had to build granaries, preservation methods and rules about who owns what. Over time, those grew into property rights, insurance, legal frameworks and communal rules.

Warm climates are more forgiving. You can replant. The gaps between harvests are shorter. So the pressure to plan far ahead is weaker.

The essay connects this to Northern Europe building insurance and property protections earlier than the Mediterranean, and to Japan's planning culture being tied to a survival imperative.

---

## Abundance, crops and colonial institutions

Abundance sounds like an advantage. The essay argues it can work against you. When wealth comes easily, from a fertile valley or from oil, a small elite can capture it without building systems that include everyone else.

Crops matter too. Wheat and barley need storage, taxation and coordination. Perishable crops like cassava and bananas need less of all three.

Then colonialism added another layer. Per the essay, Europeans settled in temperate colonies and built inclusive institutions there. In hot, disease-heavy colonies they set up extractive institutions instead, and those often outlasted independence.

---

## Mountains protect and isolate

The essay compares Italy, Colombia and Mexico. They sit at similar latitudes but have very different incomes per person. One difference is that Italy is coastal, while much of Colombia and Mexico sits in the mountains.

Highland capitals like Bogotá (about 2,600 m), Addis Ababa and Mexico City make sense as a survival response. Higher means cooler and fewer pathogens.

But mountains carry a cost. There are no navigable rivers, roads are expensive, and landslides are common. Building infrastructure gets disproportionately expensive as terrain gets rougher. Regions stay disconnected, trust across them stays low, and local conflict is more likely. Pueyo calls this fragmentation "Balkanization". The essay's examples include cartels in Colombia's mountains and wars along the Rift Valley. The state's reach stays weak.

Mountains also cut you off from maritime trade, and ports are where capital, technology, ideas and markets come in.

The author sums up this strand neatly. Mountains shield you from the climate but tax you with isolation.

---

## The exceptions are the point

If climate were destiny, there would be no hot rich countries. There are.

- **Singapore** built its own constraint through trade.
- **Dubai** bought its way around the climate with oil money and migrant labour.
- **The US South** caught up only after air conditioning became widespread.

The line from the essay I keep coming back to is that constraint can be engineered if nature doesn't provide it.

---

## What warming changes

The essay's outlook isn't comfortable. It cites the IMF estimate that a 1°C rise in hot countries cuts growth by about 1.3 percentage points a year. Tropical crop yields fall while some temperate yields rise. Dengue and malaria are spreading into southern Europe and the southern US. Heat stress may make parts of South Asia and the Middle East periodically unlivable outdoors without cooling.

The tools to respond exist already. The essay names vaccines, irrigation, trade, digital work and air conditioning. It also points to Europe's resistance to air conditioning as a warning about how institutions can drag their feet.

---

## The author's own caveats

I want to keep these, because they're what make the essay honest.

- None of the evidence is conclusive on its own.
- Heat alone can't explain the gap.
- Disease amplifies poverty. It doesn't dictate it.
- Warm climates can and do build strong institutions. The argument is about *incentive gradients*, which way the pressure pushes, not about what's possible.
- It is explicitly **not fate**.

The author's closing question for this century is whether societies can adapt their institutions and technology fast enough to offset the drag of warming.

---

## Reading it from Coimbatore

I'm reading this from Coimbatore, in India, a country where heat is part of everyday life for most people. I don't have numbers of my own to add, and I'd rather not invent any. But the framework changes which questions I ask.

The essay doesn't say a warm country can't get rich. It says a warm country has to build on purpose what a cold country was *pushed* into building. Storage, cooling, disease control, roads, institutions that plan for the long term. None of it shows up automatically.

As an engineering student, I find that oddly motivating. It frames development as a set of engineering and design problems. Keeping people cool enough to work. Keeping food and machines from rotting. Connecting regions that geography keeps apart. Building rules that reward planning over extraction.

Singapore's lesson, as the essay tells it, is that you can create the constraint yourself.

The climate sets the difficulty. It doesn't set the outcome.
