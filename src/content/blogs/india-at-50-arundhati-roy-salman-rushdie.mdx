*Notes on [India at 50, a 1997 TV conversation](https://youtu.be/tIekAj-ussc) with Salman Rushdie, Arundhati Roy and Barbara Crossette.*

It is August 1997. India is turning fifty.

A television studio in New York brings together three people to talk about it. Salman Rushdie, whose *Midnight's Children* came out in 1981 and who had just co-edited *Mirrorwork*, an anthology of Indian writing since independence. Arundhati Roy, whose debut *The God of Small Things* had come out that year to huge praise, and who has a piece in that anthology. And Barbara Crossette, then the New York Times bureau chief at the United Nations, who used to report from Delhi.

The host is never named in the clip, so I won't guess.

What makes this worth watching nearly thirty years later is that the two novelists agree on a lot and still keep landing in different places. One is looking at India from a distance he did not choose. The other has just flown in from it and is flying back that night.

---

## Is there anything to celebrate

The host opens with a stack of American newspaper headlines about India's fifty years. Rushdie finds that interesting in itself. For years, he says, people told him Americans didn't care about India. Somebody was reading his books, though, and the reception for Roy's novel suggested that the connection had finally been made.

Roy is cooler about the whole thing. Back home, she says, the headlines are less about the anniversary and more about a politics of despair. She points out the irony of the British Council putting "Britain celebrates" the anniversary on its letterhead. Independence came with a massacre still in living memory, and India is still fighting what she calls the ghosts of colonialism.

Her best line here is that India lives in several centuries at once, and those centuries argue with each other.

She describes the economy and television opening up a few years earlier. The middle class welcomed the West in and fought it at the same time.

---

## Will it hold together

The host asks whether India staying in one piece is itself the miracle.

Rushdie says he never really believed it would split. He lists the separatist pressures, including the Khalistan movement, but says none of them was ever a credible threat to break the country up. He contrasts that with Pakistan, which began as a larger country and split in two.

Then he goes further. He owns a block of silver, a gift on the day he was born, engraved with the map of undivided India. When editing *Mirrorwork* he simply ignored the border and included Pakistani writers, because he didn't see why literature should be partitioned. He is careful to say he doesn't mean political reunion. He means something looser, like trade barriers coming down, so that fifty years on people might see partition as a *blip* rather than something permanent.

Roy says she would love to feel that way. Living there, she sees something else.

On top, there is what she calls an Internet culture of MTV and television. Underneath, tribalism. Whether the country holds together politically is like asking whether a marriage still exists. The real question is whether it's a good marriage.

> There isn't a single Indian, she says, who can't claim to belong to some minority if they want to.

The fault lines keep moving. Sometimes Hindu and Muslim, sometimes Hindu and Sikh, sometimes caste, sometimes language. Her worry is that each political party drives its stakes into one group and replays the same drama.

---

## Where it went wrong

Rushdie dates the decay to the Emergency under Indira Gandhi in the mid-seventies. In his telling, that was when the restraints came off. Religious sectarianism became an open political card, the civil service lost its independence, and corruption, which had always existed, "went nuclear".

Crossette adds what she saw as a reporter. Unemployed young men were bussed in to swell political crowds. The mood at Rajiv Gandhi's funeral in 1991 was ugly in a way Indira Gandhi's in 1984 had not been. She ties it to poverty. As long as huge numbers of people lack a decent income, politics can turn frightening.

Roy's version is more personal. What scared her about the rise of the BJP was not the street mobs. It was educated, intellectual people she knew, suddenly voicing a prejudice she had never suspected was there.

She also talks about what this does to artists. There is a free press, she says, but writers and painters now know their place, because of what happened to Rushdie and because a painting by M. F. Husain had been burnt the year before.

Her own book had landed her in court. A lawyer from near where she grew up had filed a criminal case accusing her of corrupting public morality. The high court had stayed it while it decided. Her dry take is that her book offends everybody a little, and that keeps her more or less safe.

---

## Writing in English

This is the part I came for.

Rushdie gives the practical answer first. Most of the Indian middle class goes to English-medium schools. English is simply his best language, and he wouldn't write a novel in any other.

Then the interesting part. He thinks English is flexible enough to take in "other music". The way people in India speak, think and dream is different from England or America, and he wanted an English that could carry that. Every serious writer, he says, has a *linguistic project*. India let him become a writer he couldn't have become otherwise. His project was taking on "the whole damn thing", writing books that give the illusion of containing all of India, even though you couldn't fit one street into 500 pages.

Roy pushes back, gently. She is not conscious of remodelling English to tell Indian stories. It's just how she thinks. *It's mine*, she says. She suspects many newer writers feel the same. They don't experience English as a foreign language bent to a native story.

She also raises something less flattering. Many Indians speak several languages, and for a writer that can enrich your language. For many others, she says, it breaks it down, and they end up speaking no language properly. Not everyone who speaks Indian English speaks it beautifully.

Rushdie's reply is generous. Of course, he says, she is writing about small things and intimacies, and good for it. His books took on the whole thing. He jokes that he has probably finished doing that, so his next novel will be about a small thing.

So the difference isn't really English versus not-English. It's a writer who sees his English as a deliberate project and a writer who sees hers as simply her own voice.

---

## What they would keep

Crossette is the optimist of the three. She is amazed that in the smallest village at election time, people give their names and their political opinions without fear. She sees new non-governmental groups taking on single issues. Her one priority for India would be primary education over subsidies for higher education.

Rushdie answers her "one thing" with a story from *Mirrorwork*. A student in Delhi once told him *Midnight's Children* was very long and asked what its point was, fundamentally. It's a good joke about how badly a big book fits into one sentence.

Asked what she values most, Roy says the depth of human relationships. She wouldn't trade it for anything.

Rushdie agrees, and then says the saddest thing in the hour. It had been almost ten years since he could go to India. He feels he is near the end of his relationship with it and doesn't want to write about it from ignorance. His new book, he says, is about people who leave. If *Midnight's Children* was about the dream of home, this one is about the dream of going away.

He picks up Roy's marriage image. In his case, it feels like it's over. He feels like the one who got dumped.

Roy's answer is that what happened to Salman has its echoes in all of them. Everyone knows the lines they can't cross now.

---

## What I take from it

Three things stayed with me.

First, two people can share most of their values and still disagree honestly about what they see. Rushdie argues from history and from distance. Roy argues from the street she is going back to. Neither is wrong to start where they start.

Second, Roy's marriage line is a better question than "will it hold together?" Plenty of systems survive. Surviving and working well are different tests, and you have to look at the second one on purpose.

Third, the language part. I study and code in English and rarely stop to question it. Rushdie's idea that every writer needs a *linguistic project*, and Roy's answer that her English is just hers, are two ways of owning a tool you didn't choose. I like both.

The clip is short. Watch it for Roy's line about India living in several centuries at once.
