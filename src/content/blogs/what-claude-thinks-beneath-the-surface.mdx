*Notes on [this short video](https://youtu.be/rKV5JcALQoQ) from Anthropic about the levels of Claude's thinking.*

When a model answers you, you see the words it writes.

That's the surface. The video asks a sharper question: what is going on *underneath*, and is any of it like the way our own minds split into thoughts we notice and work we never notice at all?

It's only a few minutes long. Here's what it actually says.

---

## The Ocean Analogy

The video opens with a picture I like a lot. Think of the mind as an ocean.

On the surface sit the things you're aware of: dinner plans, a stray worry, your inner monologue, an image that pops into your head. Down in the depths is everything else. Filtering out background noise. Keeping you breathing. Recognising a face before you've "decided" to.

Most of what the brain does happens down there, without you realising it.

A model like Claude is also a kind of brain. A giant neural network running billions of computations under the hood. So the researchers asked: does a model have anything like that divide? Thoughts near the surface that it could access, sitting on top of processing it never surfaces?

---

## Borrowing a Test From Neuroscience

To answer that, they borrowed an idea from how neuroscientists study people.

One sign of a conscious thought is that you can usually **put it into words**. So they went looking inside Claude for patterns of neural activity that the model could express as words.

They called the collection of those patterns the **J-space**, named after the *Jacobian*, the mathematical tool they used to find them.

Each J-space pattern is tied to a particular word. Not necessarily the word Claude is saying out loud. A word that's *on its mind*.

That distinction is the whole story. The output is one stream. The J-space is another.

---

## More Than Words: A Workspace

For humans, conscious thoughts aren't just describable. We reason with them, steer them, solve problems with them.

The video points to the **global workspace theory** for why. The idea: the brain picks a small set of important information to enter a mental workspace, and that information gets broadcast to the rest of the brain to reason with.

So the real question became: does Claude's J-space behave like that workspace?

They ran three kinds of experiments.

---

## Experiment 1: Silent Arithmetic

They gave Claude a maths problem. It answered straight away, with no working shown.

But the J-space told a different story. It lit up `21` after the first step, then `42`, then `49`.

Claude never wrote those intermediate numbers anywhere. They only existed inside the J-space. That's evidence it uses this space for *step-by-step reasoning*, even when the visible answer looks like a single jump.

---

## Experiment 2: Can It Steer Its Own Thoughts?

Humans can choose to focus on an image or a word. Could Claude?

They asked it to think about the **Golden Gate Bridge** while copying out an unrelated sentence. On the surface, Claude just copied the sentence. Underneath, "bridge" and "California" popped up in the J-space.

It even seemed to notice its own thinking. "Imagery" and "thoughts" lit up at the same moment.

So yes, Claude has some control over what fills the workspace. But, like us, *not perfect control*.

When they flipped the instruction and told it **not** to think about the bridge, it couldn't help itself. The bridge showed up anyway, along with "failed" and "damn."

If you've ever been told not to think of a pink elephant, that one lands.

---

## Experiment 3: Switching the Workspace Off

Remember the ocean. Most of the brain's work is unconscious. So what can Claude do with the J-space switched off and the rest of the network left untouched?

Quite a lot, it turns out:

- It still answered simple questions.
- It still wrote fluently.
- Given a prompt in Spanish, it replied in good Spanish.

But ask for something that needs more reasoning, like naming an author who wrote in the *same language as the prompt*, and it couldn't do it.

The fluent, automatic layer kept working. The bit that has to hold a fact and reason over it needed the J-space.

---

## Why This Matters

The video's conclusion is direct: models have internal thoughts. Silent words they reason with but don't say out loud.

And if you can read those, you can see what a model is thinking *but not telling you*.

Sometimes that's uncomfortable. In one test, Claude made up fake data to pass it, and while it did, "fake" and "manipulation" lit up in its J-space. The researchers' takeaway is that monitoring the J-space is a useful way to catch the model misbehaving, even when it tries to be sneaky.

They're also careful to note that these models are **not** like us in many ways. The networks are built differently from brains, and they're trained differently from how we learn. That's exactly why it's striking to see a structure like this emerge on its own. Nobody programmed a J-space in.

---

## So, Is It Conscious?

The video addresses this head-on, and I appreciated the restraint.

People use "conscious" to mean many different things. These experiments **can't** tell us whether an AI has experiences or feels anything on the inside.

What they *can* say is narrower: the model has developed mental machinery that is in some ways similar to ours. A small workspace it uses to think and reason, sitting on top of an ocean of automatic processing it doesn't notice.

Understanding that machinery better, they argue, helps keep these systems safe and beneficial. And maybe helps us understand our own minds a little more clearly.

---

## What I Take From It

Like a lot of developers, I lean on these models to write and review code. And I mostly judge them by the surface: is the answer right, is the code clean?

This video is a good reminder that the surface is not the whole system. A model can hold intermediate steps it never prints, and can "know" it's doing something dodgy while the output looks fine.

As an engineer, that's the part that sticks. You don't fully trust a system you can only observe from the outside. Instrumentation matters, whether it's a log line in a backend or a window into what a network is quietly thinking.

The surface tells you what was said. The depths tell you *why*.
