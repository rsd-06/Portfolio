*Notes on [Justice: What's The Right Thing To Do?](https://youtu.be/kBdfcR-8hEY) by Michael Sandel, Harvard University. All twelve episodes.*

I went in expecting a philosophy lecture.

What I got was a packed hall of students raising hands, standing up to defend a position, and watching that position get turned against them ten minutes later.

That's the format. Sandel tells a story, takes a poll, asks someone to explain their vote, then changes one detail. The principle that felt obvious a moment ago suddenly doesn't. Only after the room has argued itself into a corner does he bring in the philosopher who saw it coming centuries earlier.

You can't watch it passively. Every case is aimed at you, and you notice your own answer flipping.

Each episode packs two lectures. Here's what each one covers, as the class argued it.

---

## The moral side of murder

*Episode 01. [Watch it here](https://youtu.be/kBdfcR-8hEY).*

It opens with the trolley problem. You're driving a trolley with no brakes. Five workers are ahead. You can steer onto a side track with one worker. Most of the room would turn.

Then the twist. You're on a bridge instead, next to a large man. Push him onto the track and he stops the trolley. One dies, five live. Same arithmetic.

Almost nobody would push.

Students try to explain the difference. Pushing feels like murder with your own hands, while steering feels like choosing between outcomes already in motion. Sandel keeps pressing. What if you only had to open a trap door? Still feels wrong. A doctor who saves five moderately injured patients instead of one critical one seems fine. A surgeon who takes organs from a healthy man napping next door to save five does not.

Out of this mess come two families of moral reasoning:

- **Consequentialist.** Morality lives in the results. Five beats one.
- **Categorical.** Some acts are wrong in themselves, whatever the outcome.

Jeremy Bentham's utilitarianism is the big example of the first. Immanuel Kant is the big example of the second. The course plays them against each other.

The second lecture tests Bentham on a real 19th-century case, *The Queen v. Dudley and Stephens*. The yacht Mignonette sank in the South Atlantic, and four men ended up in a lifeboat with two cans of turnips. On the nineteenth day, Captain Dudley proposed drawing lots to decide who would die. A sailor named Brooks refused. The next day Dudley and Stephens killed Richard Parker, the seventeen-year-old cabin boy, who was already ill and had no family. They survived on him until a ship rescued them, then pleaded necessity at trial.

Most of the class votes guilty. The interesting part is *why*. Some say murder is murder. Others say it would be different if Parker had consented, or if they'd held a fair lottery. Sandel turns this into three questions the course has to answer. Why is murder categorically wrong? Why would a fair procedure justify its outcome? What moral work does consent actually do?

He also warns the room that philosophy makes the familiar strange, and you can't unsee it. The easy escape is skepticism, the shrug that everyone has their own opinion. His reply is that we already live some answer to these questions every day.

## Putting a price tag on life

*Episode 02. [Watch it here](https://youtu.be/0O2Rq4HJBxw).*

Bentham's idea is simple. We are governed by **"two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure,"** so the right thing to do, for a person or a lawmaker, is whatever maximizes the overall balance of happiness over suffering.

Today that logic often goes by the name cost-benefit analysis. Sandel's examples are uncomfortable on purpose.

**Philip Morris in the Czech Republic.** The tobacco company commissioned a study showing that smoking produced a net gain of about $147 million for public finances. Part of the "benefit" was that smokers die early, saving on pensions, health care and elderly housing. The company later apologized.

**The Ford Pinto.** Ford knew the fuel tank could explode in rear collisions. The fix cost $11 a car, $137 million in total. On the other side, Ford counted 180 deaths at $200,000 each, plus injuries and burned cars, for about $49.5 million. So it skipped the fix. The jury was appalled.

One student says the problem isn't that the number was too low. It's putting a number on a life at all. Another says you have to, because decisions get made either way.

Two objections to utilitarianism come out of this:

1. **It can trample individuals.** If enough Romans enjoy watching Christians thrown to lions, does their pleasure outweigh the victims' agony?
2. **It assumes every value fits on one scale.** In the 1930s a psychologist named Thorndike asked young people on relief how much they'd need to be paid to have a tooth pulled, eat a live earthworm, or live the rest of their life in Kansas. Kansas came out most expensive. The absurdity is the point.

The second lecture brings in John Stuart Mill. Bentham had said that, for equal amounts of pleasure, **"pushpin is as good as poetry."** Mill argues some pleasures are higher, and his test is to ask people who have experienced both which they prefer.

So Sandel runs it live with a Hamlet soliloquy, a clip of *Fear Factor* and a clip of *The Simpsons*. Most of the class enjoyed The Simpsons most. Most also said Shakespeare was the higher experience. That gap between what we prefer and what we judge worthier is exactly where Mill's test wobbles.

Mill also argues that justice is the most sacred part of morality, because respecting rights makes society better off in the long run. Sandel leaves the question open. Is Mill quietly stepping outside utilitarianism to get there?

The episode ends with Bentham himself, preserved in a glass case at University College London, where board minutes record him as **"present but not voting."**

## Free to choose

*Episode 03. [Watch it here](https://youtu.be/Qw4l1w0rkjs).*

Sandel first pushes on Mill. If a doctor refuses to harvest a healthy patient's organs only because it would scare people away from checkups, is that the right reason? Or does the person deserve respect in their own right?

That opens the door to strong theories of rights, starting with libertarianism and Robert Nozick. We are separate individuals with a fundamental right to liberty, so the state may do very little. Libertarians reject three things most modern states do:

- **Paternalist laws** that protect you from yourself, like seat belt and helmet laws
- **Morals legislation** that tries to make citizens virtuous
- **Redistribution**, taxing the rich to help the poor

What's left is a minimal state for defense, police and courts.

For Nozick, you can't judge a distribution of wealth by its shape. You ask whether the original holdings were acquired fairly and whether everything since changed hands through free exchange. If both hold, the result is just, however unequal.

Sandel makes it concrete with Bill Gates and Michael Jordan. A utilitarian would tax them in a heartbeat, since a little means nothing to them and a lot to people at the bottom. A libertarian calls that coercion.

Then the chain of reasoning that makes this episode memorable. Taxing my earnings takes the fruits of my labor. That's like taking my labor itself. Claiming my labor is forced labor. And if the state owns part of my labor, it owns part of *me*. Underneath it all sits one premise, **self-ownership**.

A student named Victoria goes straight for that foundation. Maybe you don't fully own yourself once you choose to live in a society.

In the second lecture, a "team libertarian" of three students answers the class's objections. The poor need the money more? Need doesn't justify violating a right, and needing something isn't the same as deserving it. Taxes in a democracy have consent? One vote among millions isn't deciding what to do with your own property. The successful owe society a debt? They already repaid it through what they gave others in free exchange.

The episode closes by tracing self-ownership to its source, John Locke.

## This land is my land

*Episode 04. [Watch it here](https://youtu.be/MGyygiXMzRk).*

At first Locke looks like the libertarian's best ally. He believes in natural rights to life, liberty and property that exist before any government, and that no government can override.

His account of property is the famous part. You own yourself, so you own your labor. Mix your labor with something unowned, like picking fruit or tilling land, and it becomes yours, provided there's enough, and as good, left for others.

The catch is that Locke's rights are **unalienable**. Sandel compares them to a non-transferable ticket. It's yours, but you can't sell it. For Locke, you can't take your own life or sell yourself into slavery. So you don't own yourself in the absolute way libertarians want.

The class pushes on the property theory too. A student named Rachelle argues it conveniently justifies settlers taking land from Native Americans who didn't enclose it. Others reply that, on Locke's own terms, hunting on and using common land might count as labor, so the argument could cut the other way.

The second lecture turns to **consent**. Why leave the state of nature? Because everyone is their own judge and enforcer, and people judging their own cases overreact. So we agree to form a government and accept majority rule.

Here Locke gets slippery. He says government can't take your property without your consent, but also that taxes need only the consent of the majority. Sandel's reading is that *that* there is property is natural and protected. *What counts* as property, and as taking it, is for government to define.

What makes a law legitimate is that it's general, not arbitrary. Singling out Bill Gates to fund a war would be illegitimate. A general tax wouldn't. One student argues a conscription lottery works the same way.

So libertarians get two disappointments. Unalienable rights mean you don't fully own yourself. And a government built on consent turns out to be less limited than they hoped.

Sandel also notes a darker angle. Locke wrote that in the beginning "all the world was America," and he was a colonial administrator. A theory of property that needs no consent may have suited that context.

## Hired guns

*Episode 05. [Watch it here](https://youtu.be/8yT4RZy1t3s).*

Now the question is the limits of consent in markets.

Sandel starts with the US military missing recruitment targets during the Iraq war. He offers three fixes. Raise pay, bring back the draft, or hire mercenaries. The class overwhelmingly picks higher pay.

Then history. In the Civil War you could be drafted and pay someone to go in your place. People ran newspaper ads offering $500 or more. Andrew Carnegie reportedly paid a substitute less than he spent on cigars in a year. Most of the class calls this unjust, for two reasons:

1. **Coercion.** A poor man taking the money isn't choosing freely. He's taking the best of a few bad options.
2. **Civic duty.** Military service shouldn't be just another job, because it's tied to citizenship.

Then a student named Emily turns the discussion. If the Civil War system is unfair because the poor end up fighting, doesn't today's all-volunteer army do the same thing, nationwide? Sandel adds that "volunteer army" is a misnomer. It's a *paid* army. Follow market choice consistently and you end up with mercenaries. Take patriotism seriously and it argues for a draft.

The second lecture moves the question to reproduction. A college newspaper ad offered $50,000 for an egg from a tall, athletic, high-scoring donor. A commercial sperm bank accepted under 5% of applicants.

Then **Baby M**. Mary Beth Whitehead signed a contract to carry a child for William and Elizabeth Stern for a $10,000 fee. After the birth she wanted to keep the baby. Most of the class said enforce it. A deal is a deal.

The objections were that she couldn't have known, before the child existed, how she would feel, and that the whole thing looks a lot like selling a baby. The New Jersey Supreme Court used both. It voided the contract, gave custody to the father in the child's interest, restored the mother's rights, and said some things in a civilized society money can't buy.

Philosopher Elizabeth Anderson sharpens the second objection. Some goods are wrongly valued if we treat them only as things to use. Respect, love, honor and awe are other ways of valuing, and a market pushes them aside.

## Mind your motive

*Episode 06. [Watch it here](https://youtu.be/8rv-4aUbZxQ).*

Finally, Kant, whom Sandel calls the hardest philosopher in the course.

Kant agrees we feel pain and pleasure. But what sets us apart is our capacity for reason and free action, and his idea of freedom is strict. Chasing your desires isn't freedom, because you didn't choose your thirst. Sandel's example of the opposite of freedom is the old Sprite slogan, **"obey your thirst."**

To be free is to act **autonomously**, by a law you give yourself. Acting on desire or outside forces is **heteronomy**. A falling billiard ball isn't free. It just follows physics.

So what gives an action moral worth is the **motive**, not the result. Only acting from duty, doing the right thing because it's right, counts.

Kant's example is a shopkeeper who gives an inexperienced customer the correct change only because cheating might hurt his reputation. Right action, wrong reason, no moral worth. Sandel finds modern versions, like a Better Business Bureau ad selling honesty as the most profitable policy.

A thirteen-year-old spelling bee champion confessed that he'd actually misspelled the winning word. Part of his reason, he said, was **"I didn't want to feel like a slime."** Does that ruin it? A student named Judith says no. Other feelings can come along, as long as duty is the reason you act.

Kant states his supreme principle, the **categorical imperative**, in two formulas covered here:

1. **Universal law.** Act only on a principle you could will everyone to follow. A false promise to borrow $100 fails, because if everyone did it nobody would believe promises at all.
2. **Humanity as an end.** Treat people, yourself included, never merely as a means, always also as an end.

A student says the first one sounds like consequences. Mill made the same criticism. Sandel's answer is that it's a test, not a reason. It checks whether you're putting your own needs above everyone else's.

Another student asks whether buying food from a cashier counts as using them. No, says Sandel, as long as you deal with them in a way that respects their dignity.

## A lesson in lying

*Episode 07. [Watch it here](https://youtu.be/KqzW0eHzDSQ).*

The episode opens by tying up Kant. How can duty and freedom go together? A student named Matt answers for him. The moral law is one you give yourself, so obeying it isn't obeying anyone else. Then why would everyone's conscience reach the same law? Because, a student named Kelly says, it isn't Matt or Kelly doing the willing. It's pure reason, and reason is the same in all of us.

To explain how that's possible, Kant splits our experience into two standpoints. As objects in the world, we're pushed around by cause and effect, hunger and pain. As rational subjects, we can act on a law we give ourselves. Because we live in both at once, there's always a gap between what we do and what we ought to do. And because morality sits in the second standpoint, no science of the empirical world can settle a moral question.

Then the hardest case. The French writer Benjamin Constant asked Kant about a murderer at the door, asking whether your friend is hiding inside. Surely you can lie to him? Kant said no. Once you start carving out exceptions for consequences, you've given up the whole framework.

Sandel tries to defend Kant on Kant's own terms. Is there a way to protect your friend without lying? A student named John offers one: "I don't know where he is." He might have left the closet, after all. That's strictly true, and still meant to mislead.

So is there a moral difference between a lie and a misleading truth? Sandel's lighter example is an ugly tie you've been given as a gift. A white lie says "it's beautiful." A misleading truth says **"I've never seen a tie like that before."** Then the heavier one: Bill Clinton's carefully worded denials about Monica Lewinsky, which became a real argument at the impeachment hearings.

A student named Diana defends the distinction. You told the truth and wanted to be believed. A student named Wesley says the motive is the same either way, to throw the other person off. Sandel's answer for Kant is that the careful evasion still **pays homage to duty**. You can't control whether the murderer is fooled. What you can control is staying inside the moral law while you hope he is.

The second lecture moves to Kant's politics. Just laws come from a social contract, but not an actual one. Real people at a real convention bring different interests and unequal bargaining power, and what they agree to just reflects that. Kant's contract is "an idea of reason": a law is just if a whole people *could* have agreed to it.

That raises an obvious question. What is the moral force of a contract that never happened? Here Sandel brings in John Rawls, who, like Kant, rejected utilitarianism. Rawls writes that each person has **"an inviolability founded on justice that even the welfare of society as a whole cannot override."** His device is the **veil of ignorance**: choose the principles to govern society without knowing your race, class, strengths, health or place in it.

To see why a hypothetical contract might beat a real one, Sandel looks at how real contracts bind us. If I promise you $100 for 100 lobsters, you deliver them and I eat them, I owe you because I **benefited**. But what if I cancel two minutes later, before you've done anything? A student named Julian says the deal still holds. Another, Adam, puts it best: backing out cheapens the whole idea of taking an obligation on yourself.

So contracts carry weight for two separate reasons: **autonomy**, the obligation I chose, and **reciprocity**, the benefit I got. Sandel then argues that neither one is enough on its own.

- **Consent isn't sufficient.** His two sons traded baseball cards, and the older one knew more about their value, so Sandel made himself approve every trade. An 84-year-old widow in Chicago agreed to pay $50,000 to fix a leaky toilet. She agreed. It wasn't fair.
- **Consent isn't even necessary.** David Hume mocked the social contract as a philosophical fiction. Years later a painter repaired and painted a house Hume owned without his agreement. Hume refused to pay, lost in court, and paid.

Sandel's own story makes the same point. Stranded with a dead car at a rest stop in Hammond, Indiana, he met Sam of Sam's Mobile Repair, who started poking under the steering column and then billed him for the hour. Sandel said he'd never hired him. Had Sam fixed the car, Sandel agrees he'd have owed him, but because of the benefit, not because of any agreement.

Real contracts fall short of both ideals, through unequal power and unequal knowledge. Imagine one among people equal in both, and you have Rawls's veil of ignorance. What it would produce is next.

## What's a fair start?

*Episode 08. [Watch it here](https://youtu.be/VcL66zx_6No).*

Now Rawls's answer. What would people choose behind the veil of ignorance?

Not utilitarianism. Not knowing who you'll be, you wouldn't risk ending up in an oppressed minority, so you'd want **equal basic liberties** first: speech, assembly, religion, conscience. Utilitarianism, Rawls writes, fails to take seriously the distinction between persons.

For money and power you might first ask for strict equality, just to be safe. But you could do better than that, even from the bottom. So Rawls's second principle is the **difference principle**: inequalities are allowed only if they work to the benefit of the least well off. Michael Jordan's $31 million a year is fine only inside a system that makes it help the people at the bottom too.

A student named Mike pushes back. Why reason from the bottom and not the top? Behind the veil he'd pick a system that rewards effort, the way Harvard admits on merit. A student named Kate asks whether getting into Harvard is really about effort. Sandel cites a study of 146 selective colleges: only 3% of students come from the bottom quarter of the income scale, and over 70% come from affluent families.

Then Rawls's second, more straightforwardly moral argument. Shares shouldn't depend on things that are **arbitrary from a moral point of view**. He walks through four systems:

1. **Feudal aristocracy.** Your life is set by the accident of birth.
2. **Free market with formal equal opportunity.** Careers are open to talents, but people start the race from very different places.
3. **Fair meritocracy.** Schools and programmes bring everyone to the same starting line. But then the fastest runners win, and being born fast isn't your doing either.
4. **The democratic conception.** Don't make the fast runners wear lead shoes. Let them run, but change the terms on which they keep the winnings, so the lucky gain only in ways that also help those who lost out.

Mike answers with effort: bring Michael Jordan in and hear how hard his road was. Rawls's reply is that even effort depends on circumstances you can't claim credit for. Sandel tests it live. He asks who in the hall is first-born. Most hands go up, Mike's and Sandel's included. Psychologists link birth order with striving. Is it your doing that you were born first?

The second lecture opens with pay. A school teacher earns about $40,000. David Letterman earned $31 million. A Supreme Court justice like Sandra Day O'Connor earned just under $200,000, while Judge Judy, a TV judge, earned $25 million. Is that fair? For Rawls, it depends on whether the system taxes those earnings to help the least well off.

Then three objections to the difference principle.

- **Incentives.** Won't high taxes stop people working? A student named Tim answers for Rawls. Incentives are allowed, but the test is their effect on those at the bottom, not on the total size of the pie.
- **Self-ownership.** Nozick's charge that taxing earnings is theft. Rawls doesn't take it on directly, but his argument implies we don't own ourselves in that complete sense. The first principle still protects our basic liberties. What we lack is a privileged claim to whatever our talents fetch in a market.
- **Effort and desert.** Two construction workers build the same wall, one in an hour, the other in three days. Nobody thinks the slow one deserves more pay. So meritocracy really rewards contribution, and contribution leads straight back to talents nobody chose.

Milton Friedman wrote that **"life is not fair,"** and that trying to fix what nature made only leads to levelling. Rawls's answer is one of the best lines in the course: **"The natural distribution is neither just nor unjust... These are simply natural facts. What is just and unjust is the way that institutions deal with these facts."**

Last comes a distinction between **moral desert** and **entitlement**. If I win the lottery, I'm entitled to the money, but I didn't deserve to win. Rawls says distributive justice is about entitlements under fair rules, not about what anyone deserves.

There's also a second piece of luck: living in a society that happens to prize what you're good at. Letterman's kind of joke pays well here. In a litigious society it helps to do well on law school tests. In a hunting or warrior society, many of our talents would get us nowhere, and we'd be no less worthy. We're entitled to what the rules promise us. But it's a conceit, Sandel says, to think we deserve a society that values what we happen to have.

The same question applies to seats at elite universities, which is where the next episode starts.

## Arguing affirmative action

*Episode 09. [Watch it here](https://youtu.be/AUhReMT5uqA).*

Now Rawls's question about desert moves from money to opportunity: who gets a seat.

**Cheryl Hopwood** worked her way through community college and California State University, Sacramento, with a 3.8 average, then applied to the University of Texas Law School. She was turned down. The school counted race and ethnicity in admissions, saying 40% of Texans were African-American or Mexican-American. Minority applicants with her grades and test scores were admitted. She sued, saying she'd been rejected for being white. The hall splits roughly in half.

A student named Bri defends her. She can't control her race, so it shouldn't count against her. Others answer, and three arguments for affirmative action come out:

1. **Corrective.** A student named Anisha points out that minority students often went to worse-funded schools, so their scores understate their promise. This one still says only academic promise should count. It just reads the numbers more accurately.
2. **Compensatory.** A student named David calls it a temporary repair for slavery and segregation. A student named Kate replies that past wrongs shouldn't decide admissions today, and the fix is better schools, not adjusted results.
3. **Diversity.** A student named Hannah says a mixed class educates everyone, and adds that white applicants have long had their own affirmative action, through connections and **legacy admissions**.

A student named Ted invokes Martin Luther King's hope that people be judged by **"the content of their character,"** not the colour of their skin. A student named Da replies that you can't control which family you're born into either, and that shapes your grades too.

Sandel sorts the objections. The compensatory argument has to explain why Hopwood, who wronged no one, should pay for history. That needs some idea of collective responsibility across time. The diversity argument avoids that, and it's the one Harvard made to the Supreme Court in the 1978 *Bakke* case: race can count as a plus, **"just as coming from Iowa may count, or being a good middle linebacker or pianist."**

But diversity serves the common good, so a rights-minded critic asks whether Hopwood's rights were sacrificed for it. Hannah's reply is that nobody has a right to be admitted on any particular criteria. A school defines its mission, and only then do you know which qualities count. That's Rawls again: entitlement, not desert.

The second lecture tests that reply. Can a school define its mission any way it likes? In the 1950s the University of Texas Law School admitted only white students, because Texas law firms didn't hire African-Americans. In the 1930s Harvard capped Jewish admissions, and its president said part of Harvard's mission was to train Wall Street stockbrokers and senators. Is there a difference in principle?

Hannah says yes: inclusion versus exclusion. A student named Stevie adds that the old policies carried malice, a judgment that some people were worth less. Today's don't.

Sandel then reads the letters a school would have to send if it took that theory seriously. To the rejected: it's not your fault that **"society happened not to need the qualities you had to offer."** To the admitted: you have the traits society needs right now, so **"we propose to exploit your assets for society's advantage,"** and you're to be congratulated only as a lottery winner is. The hall laughs, because it feels wrong.

That's the bigger question. Libertarians, Kant and Rawls all agree that justice should *not* be about rewarding virtue or desert, and they aren't all egalitarians, so equality can't be their only reason. They seem to fear that tying justice to virtue threatens freedom.

So the course turns to the philosopher who disagrees: **Aristotle**. For him justice is giving people their due, fitting persons to roles by the relevant excellence. Who should get the best flutes? The best flute players. Not the rich, the well-born or the handsome, and not by lottery.

His reason isn't that we'd get better music and everyone would enjoy it. That would be utilitarian. It's that flutes are *for* being played well. You reason from the thing's purpose, its **telos**, so this is called **teleological** reasoning. The same instinct says the best tennis courts should go to the best tennis players, not to senior faculty.

It sounds old-fashioned, since modern science dropped purposes from nature. Sandel admits children have to be taught out of it, and reads Winnie the Pooh hearing a buzzing in a tree: the only reason for a buzzing noise is a bee, the only reason for a bee is making honey, and the only reason for honey is so that he can eat it.

But wasn't the affirmative action debate really an argument about the purpose of a university? Aristotle would say you can't think about justice any other way.

## The good citizen

*Episode 10. [Watch it here](https://youtu.be/MuiazbyOSqQ).*

Aristotle takes flute-style reasoning into politics. For him distributive justice was mainly about offices and honours, not income. To know who should rule, ask what politics is *for*.

His answer is that the city exists for the good life, forming character and cultivating virtue. Otherwise, he writes, law becomes **"a mere covenant, a guarantor of men's rights against one another."** That's exactly what Kant and Rawls want politics to be: a fair framework that leaves us free to choose our own ends.

Why would a good life need politics at all? Two reasons.

- **It's our nature.** Humans have language, the capacity to deliberate about just and unjust, and only in a political community do we use it fully. Someone who lives outside one, Aristotle says, **"must be either a beast or a god."**
- **Virtue is learned by doing.** You can't learn it from a book, any more than you can learn the flute, cooking or comedy from one. Each is about getting the hang of it, reading the particulars of a situation, and no rule can teach that. Civic virtue comes from deliberating with fellow citizens.

So those with the most civic virtue, like Pericles, should have the most say. Partly because they'll decide well, but also because part of politics is to **honour** that excellence.

The modern test case is **Casey Martin**, a top golfer with a circulatory condition that makes walking hard and dangerous. He asked the PGA for a golf cart in tournaments, was refused, and sued under the Americans with Disabilities Act. Most of the hall sides with him.

A student named Tommy says walking has been part of golf since it began, so it's essential at the top level. A student named Reva points out that the Senior PGA Tour, the Nike Tour and many college events allow carts, and Martin still walks about a mile anyway. The Supreme Court ruled for Martin, citing testimony that walking a course burns fewer calories than a Big Mac. Justice Scalia dissented. Golf, like any game, has no purpose beyond amusement, he wrote, so no court can say what's essential to it. He quoted Mark Twain: golf is **"a good walk spoiled."**

Sandel's read is that two things are under the surface. One is purpose: is walking essential? The other is honour. Professional golfers like Jack Nicklaus testified against the cart. If golf can be played sitting down, is it an athletic contest, or a game of skill like billiards? The difference between a sport and a spectacle is that a sport calls forth and honours certain excellences.

In the second lecture a student named Jenny asks why not let everyone ride a cart. Da answers that it would be like letting every Olympic swimmer use flippers. A student named Michael, who admits he usually rides a cart himself, suggests Martin play in a separate competition. Jenny says the court found that only swinging the club is essential.

Then the hard objection: does fitting people to roles leave room for freedom? Aristotle defended slavery. It was necessary, he argued, so that citizens could be free to deliberate, and some people were, he claimed, fit by nature to be ruled. Sandel calls it a deplorable passage. But Aristotle himself admitted many Athenian slaves were simply captives of war, so by his own test of fit that slavery was unjust. Needing coercion to keep someone in a role is a sign the role doesn't fit them.

Students push harder. One jokes that if you walk and talk like a pirate, Aristotle would send you to sea, not to investment banking. Mary Kate says she might be the world's best janitor and still want to do something else. Patrick says the golf debate shows we'll never agree on purposes, so justice can't rest on them.

That's the modern liberal case in one line: because people disagree about the good life, justice should stay neutral about it. To settle it, the course has to ask whether the right comes before the good, and what it means to be free.

## The claims of community

*Episode 11. [Watch it here](https://youtu.be/iOotE9_OGGs).*

Kant's reply to Aristotle is that basing law on any one idea of the good life risks coercion. Laws should set a fair framework of rights and let people choose their own ends. Behind that is a picture of the person as a **free, independent self**, bound by no tie of history, tradition or status it didn't choose. It's a liberating picture. We're the authors of the only obligations that bind us.

The communitarian critics say it misses something. Alasdair MacIntyre argues that humans are storytelling creatures: I can only answer "what am I to do?" if I can answer **"of what story or stories do I find myself a part?"** I'm someone's son or daughter, a citizen of this city, a member of this nation. I inherit debts and expectations with that history. MacIntyre calls it moral shallowness for an American to say "I never owned any slaves," or a German born after 1945 to say the Nazi past has nothing to do with him.

So on top of the liberal's two kinds of obligation, **natural duties** we owe everyone and **voluntary obligations** we've agreed to, the communitarian adds a third: obligations of **solidarity and membership**. Sandel's examples:

- Two children are drowning and you can save one, yours or a stranger's. Would you flip a coin?
- You didn't choose your parents, yet you seem to owe more to your own aging parents than to strangers'.
- In the Second World War a French Resistance pilot refused one bombing target because it was his own village, though he didn't dispute that the raid was necessary.
- During a famine in Ethiopia, Israel airlifted several hundred Ethiopian Jews.
- There's a Franklin, Texas, and a Franklin, Mexico, across the Rio Grande. Do Americans owe more to the needy in one than in the other?

The critics answer. Patrick worries that our communities overlap, so their claims will conflict, with no way to choose. A student named Nicola says humanity, the widest community, should always come first. Patrick replies it could just as well be the narrowest, your family. A student named Elizabeth calls citizenship an accident of birth, like which side of the river you were born on. A student named Reena compares patriotism to the house pride that first-year students feel within a day of being sorted: sentiment, not obligation.

The second lecture tests loyalty against justice. A student named Julia notes that Rawls himself says ordinary citizens have no political obligation, strictly speaking, beyond what they choose. A student named AJ Kumar says he owes more to his country than to humanity for the same reason he owes more to his parents. A German student named Ike replies that if he'd been born 80 years earlier, he wouldn't owe anything to Nazi Germany for having benefited from it. A student named Marina asks whether communal obligation means writing your country **"a blank moral check."** A student named Rahul says no: love of country is what keeps you in the argument, instead of leaving it to fundamentalists.

Sandel sets the real test: a case where loyalty competes with a universal duty and wins. A student named Dan supplies one. If he saw his roommate cheating on a problem set, he wouldn't turn him in. Many in the hall agree.

Then bigger versions. Billy Bulger, president of the University of Massachusetts, refused to tell a grand jury where his fugitive brother Whitey, an alleged gang leader wanted by the FBI, was hiding: **"I do have a loyalty to my brother."** And Robert E. Lee opposed secession, but turned down command of the Union army: **"I have not been able to make up my mind to raise my hand against my relatives, my children, my home."** Whether or not Lee chose rightly, Sandel says, we can't even see it as a moral dilemma unless loyalty carries moral weight.

A student named Andre notices that all three chose the closer community, and suggests the most particular always wins. A student named Samantha points out that in the same Civil War, brothers chose opposite sides. Without some further moral judgment, the size of a community can't settle it.

The episode ends on the danger. If justice depends on a community's shared understandings, as Michael Walzer argues, what about a community whose shared understanding is segregation? Sandel plays a clip from *Eyes on the Prize* of white Southerners in the 1950s defending their tradition as a way of life. Does tying justice to the good leave it at the mercy of local custom?

## Debating same-sex marriage

*Episode 12. [Watch it here](https://youtu.be/EzD9P-9sj4M).*

The final episode is where Sandel finally argues his own view, in three steps.

**First, obligations of solidarity are real.** The universal view says the widest loyalty should always win and special concern for friends is a kind of prejudice. Montesquieu followed that honestly to its end: **"If men were perfectly virtuous, they wouldn't have friends."** Sandel's problem with that world isn't that it's unrealistic. It's that it would be hard to recognise as a human one. We learn to love humanity through its particular expressions.

**Second, that doesn't hand justice to local custom.** There are two ways to tie justice to the good. The relativist way says justice is whatever a community's traditions say, which leaves you nothing to say to the segregationists. The other way says a right is justified by the worth of the human good it serves. That keeps justice critical, but it means we have to argue about the good, in a society that disagrees about it.

**Third, that argument is unavoidable.** To show it, the class debates same-sex marriage.

Two students, Mark and Ryan, argue against it from a teleological view of sex and marriage, as a Catholic in Mark's case: their purposes are procreation and the union of husband and wife. Hannah asks about infertile couples. Mark replies that sex has purposes beyond procreation, so older couples can marry. A student named Steve separates two questions: whether something is permitted, and whether the state should honour it with marriage. Hannah, pressed, says she'd allow consensual polygamy and even marrying yourself.

A student named Victoria says the state shouldn't impose one religion's idea of marriage, and should offer civil unions instead. A student named Sezanne goes further: the state shouldn't recognise any marriages at all.

In the second lecture, a student named Andrea, also a Catholic, says she only came to support same-sex marriage after deciding for herself, through thought, prayer and argument, that homosexuality isn't a sin. A student named Daniel says you can separate the two: he thinks abortion is morally wrong but still supports keeping it legal.

Sandel turns to the Massachusetts *Goodridge* ruling that legalised same-sex marriage there. It starts out neutral: what's at stake is **"respect for individual autonomy and equality under law,"** not the moral worth of the choice. But a truly neutral state would follow the writer Michael Kinsley and take government out of marriage altogether. The court didn't. It praised marriage as one of the community's **"most rewarding and cherished institutions,"** and wrote that every civil marriage has three partners: **"two willing spouses and an approving state."** That's honour, not neutrality. And it argued about purpose directly, concluding that the point of marriage is not procreation but the exclusive, permanent commitment of the partners.

Sandel stresses that none of this argues for or against same-sex marriage. It argues against the idea that you can decide it while staying neutral on the moral questions underneath.

So is reasoning about the good possible? Not if it means plugging in a single rule. But it's what the course has been doing all along, moving back and forth between judgments about particular cases and the principles that make sense of them, sometimes revising one, sometimes the other. Rawls himself calls this **reflective equilibrium**, and writes that **"moral philosophy is Socratic."** Rawls applies it only to justice, because people reasonably disagree about the good. Sandel's reply is that they reasonably disagree about justice too, libertarians against egalitarians, and we argue those out anyway.

What about respect for those we disagree with? The liberal kind of respect sets their convictions aside. Sandel argues for a different kind: engaging with them, sometimes challenging them, sometimes learning from them. There's no guarantee it leads to agreement. Learning more about a belief can make you like it less. But it suits a pluralist society better.

He closes where he began, with Kant: scepticism is **"a resting place for human reason... but it is no dwelling place for permanent settlement."** The aim of the course, he says, was to awaken the restlessness of reason.

## What I take from it

The biggest effect of these twelve lectures wasn't learning who said what. It was noticing how fast my own reasoning shifts when one detail of a case changes.

I build systems and train ML models. A lot of that work is, in effect, Bentham with a loss function. Pick an objective, add everything up into one number, maximize it. Usually that's fine. Often it's the only workable approach.

But the Pinto memo stuck with me. Its problem was never the arithmetic. It was what got counted, and the decision that a life could be one more entry in the sum.

Thorndike's survey shows the same trap from another side. Once everything sits on one scale, you can rank anything against anything, and the ranking will be confidently absurd.

So the lesson isn't "don't optimize." It's narrower:

- **Know what your single number hides.** A model that does well on average can still fail a small group badly. That's the minority-rights objection, applied to an accuracy score.
- **Some limits aren't weights.** Some constraints shouldn't be traded away for enough benefit. They belong outside the objective, as hard rules.
- **Judge a system from the bottom.** Rawls asks how a society treats its least well off. The same question works for a model: look at the worst-served group, not just the average.
- **Neutral is rarely on offer.** The marriage ruling tried to stay neutral and couldn't. A default, a metric or a filter already takes a view about what matters, so it's better to say which view.
- **The reason matters, not just the result.** A system that behaves only because misbehaving would be noticed is Kant's shopkeeper. Worth remembering when we judge models by outputs alone.

None of these lectures hands you a final answer, and Sandel doesn't pretend they do. What they gave me is a habit. When a trade-off looks obviously right, I change one detail and check whether I still believe it.
